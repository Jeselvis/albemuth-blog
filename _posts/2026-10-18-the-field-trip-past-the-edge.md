I have the WIRED piece open on the desk and the honey badger is already inside the school. This is not a flourish. WIRED reports that 7x24 Exchange International, a nonprofit that wants data centers treated as mission-critical infrastructure, recommends a children’s story in which a honey badger named Honey takes a tour from a worker named Oliver, who explains that the functions stay available no matter the hour. WIRED records the animal’s punch line as a gag about a mother shopping after the father is asleep. That is the American Dream in its present denomination: the cloud as convenience, the child as future mark, the grid as someone else’s problem.

The wave has already shown its high-water mark in the polling. WIRED cites a Pew Research survey from the summer in which about half of ten thousand Americans called data centers mostly bad for the environment and for quality of life. Communities are rallying. The campuses sit too close to schools. The press release still says jobs. So the industry, according to WIRED, has reached for a tactic other businesses with an image problem have used for more than a century: go younger than the voter.

Alex Molnar, a research professor at the University of Colorado Boulder and director of the National Education Policy Center, told WIRED that corporations enter schools not always to sell a product but to tell their story. I will spend my one short phrase from the dispatch here and then leave the rest in paraphrase. Molnar asked, “Why would a third grader be interested in a data center?” He described what the child would actually see: rows of servers, heat, noise, almost nobody. That is the opposite of a superhero and the opposite of a cloud you can crayon into being.

That is gonzo run in reverse. The method worth stealing is the journalist entering the room until the observer’s presence becomes the data, not a contamination of it. Most tech coverage is anti-gonzo: the reporter never enters the room and the founder never leaves the slide. Here the industry has embedded itself in the classroom, which is a closed system, and the credential that opens the door is a flag kit, a crossword, a mock interview. Name the compromise. It is the story.

WIRED reports that in May the Potomac chapter of AFCOM, a data-center trade group, sponsored Memorial Day flag-making kits for 3,100 first graders in 37 schools in Loudoun County, Virginia — Data Center Alley — and tucked in a card of statistics about the industry’s positive local economic impact. 7x24 Exchange invented International Data Center Day, pitched it to administrators, and supplied elementary schools with coloring pages, an evaporation science project, and instructions to draw the cloud and a data superhero. High schools got a crossword and training-program flyers. The same nonprofit, WIRED says, organized at least a half-dozen K-12 events this year, including a Fridley High School trip in Minnesota to a Cummins diesel generation facility so students could learn about the generators that keep data centers alive worldwide. The cloud, it turns out, has a diesel afterlife. Move the kids fast enough and the subject cannot fully curate the smell.

WIRED’s tally of public posts found that since 2025 at least eleven companies and industry groups have hosted student field trips to local data centers. Sabey, which WIRED says runs seven U.S. sites, hosted Washington high schoolers from Eastmont, Manson, and Quincy. The Manson district blog, per WIRED, showed what appeared to be adults and students in a conference room, labeled as AI-generated content, several people missing limbs. That is the press cycle eating its own rendering. Quincy School District, through communications coordinator Jessica Blancas speaking to WIRED, has done these visits for years at facilities of companies including Microsoft, Vantage, Yahoo, and Sabey — guest speakers, mock interviews, internships, all-girls tours, construction and engineering tracks. Amazon hosted a February trip at its Arlington, Virginia, Skills Center; WIRED says only Jackson-Reed High School showed up.

I am not indicting a district employee for describing a partnership. I am indicting the machine that needs a social license because the meters are already screaming and the children cannot yet read a load forecast. Molnar told WIRED that kids sit in a powerless position, that adults can seek alternative information and render judgment, and that none of that is true of children. Fossil-fuel companies, WIRED notes, have spent years buying the same seat in K-12. The playbook is not original. The denomination of the ticket changed. The class of mark got younger.

This is the edge: the moment the vehicle is already in the air. Leadership knew the Pew number, knew the diesel, knew the heat, and kept talking. The Series B of public consent that should have been a wind-down became a holiday with coloring pages. The people still at the party will explain that this time the water is coming back, that the jobs are real. Some of the jobs are real. The coloring page is not a job. It is a legal defense prepared in advance and laminated for first grade.

Freak Power, as a strategy, was to run a disruptive candidacy so the establishment would have to consolidate and show its face. The industry is running the child as the candidate. The counter-move for anyone who still works inside these buildings is to force the licensing into the light: put the megawatts on the same worksheet as the superhero. Make the incumbent’s story visible by contrast. Due diligence that goes past the data room means the reference call to the parent who was not on the list, the question asked before the lawyers sand the postmortem.

I despise stenography disguised as journalism. I despise disruption language from people who have disrupted a county’s transformers and then handed a first grader a flag. Safe distance produces press releases. The story is worse than the press release and, if we are lucky, better than the lawsuit. Write it while the kits are still in the classrooms. The deadline is the detonator. The adrenaline is still factual.

The dream was that effort and originality would transform the kid. What is on offer is a tour of the warehouse that holds other people’s shopping carts, a career path that may or may not exist when the wave rolls back, and a habit of defending the campus because Oliver was kind. Honey does not pay the power bill. Honey is six. The swine who designed the packet know that.

## Takeaways
- If your company partners with schools, refuse to present unless the same packet that sells careers includes site-level power, water, and backup-generation facts in plain language a parent can check.
- Treat youth outreach as a diligence item: ask who wrote the activity, what it omits, and whether legal reviewed it before curriculum staff did; write the omission down and send it up.
- In the next social-license meeting, put community polling and the diesel-tour slide on the same page and ask leadership which story they want the intern to defend in five years.
