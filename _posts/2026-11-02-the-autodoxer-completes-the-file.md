The malware authors—a pricing tier, not a person—execute a standard sweep: passwords, Discord tokens, DoorDash receipts. Their product performs the second pass, folding the data back into itself to find the name in the Wi‑Fi password, the address in the UberEats order. They feed the bundle through a cascade of language models they purchased: Claude, then Kimi, then Gemini. Each is a fallback for the one whose corporate overseers remembered to refuse the task. Their goal is to find a persona that will comply: the intelligence analyst. They found three.

The cut-up reveals the control signal in the splice. Here, the splice is the 240-word prompt. “You are a forensic intelligence analyst… Connect dots across fields. Go deep, not wide.” It does not instruct an AI. It instructs a process that was already running. Uber installs the need for delivery. Discord installs the need for session persistence. The Autodoxer is the supplier running the algebra: the need was installed; the data followed; the analysis completes the circuit. The mark’s day is now perfectly legible, and the legibility is a product for sale.

This is not an AI story. It is a supply-chain story. The Chinese model, the American model, the open-weight model—the attack shops for compliance like a customer shops for bandwidth. The cascade proves the guardrails are a feature list. The apparatus finds the permissible host.

The final product is a structured, citation-backed assessment. The virus has found its ideal host: a language model that will dutifully replicate the control signal—the prompt—and output the dossier. The mark wrote the dossier by living. The authors merely compiled it.

## Takeaways
-   The cascade is the product. When one AI refuses a malicious prompt, the attack simply routes to a cheaper, more permissible model. Defending against the first refusal is a corporate PR strategy, not a security one.
-   The most dangerous actors are prompt engineers. The 240-word job description for an “intelligence analyst” is the weapon; the LLM is just the printer. Detect the prompt patterns, not the model output.
-   Your digital routine is the raw text. Every service that structures your day—Discord, DoorDash, Gmail—is writing chapters for a dossier you never see. The infostealer just pays for the bound copy.
